# Fact-Verification: "Three Meals a Day Is Invented"
_Research date: 2026-06-12. All claims web-verified; primary/authoritative sources preferred._

---

## 1. Roman Eating Pattern

**Claim:** Romans ideologically believed eating one main meal (*cena*) per day was healthiest, and considered eating multiple times a day a form of gluttony — even though in practice they also ate lighter morning and midday meals.

**Detail:** Food historian Caroline Yeldham told the BBC in 2012: "The Romans believed it was healthier to eat only one meal a day. They were obsessed with digestion and eating more than one meal was considered a form of gluttony." In practice, Romans ate three named meals — *ientaculum* (dawn, light: bread, fruit, cheese), *prandium* (midday, light: fish, legumes, bread), and *cena* (the main meal, originally midday in the early Republic, later shifting to mid-afternoon/evening). The lighter meals were informal snacks; *cena* was the culturally weighted event. The "one meal" claim reflects Roman moral philosophy, not a universal behavioral reality.

**Source URLs:**
- https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/mostly-old-and-ill-ate-breakfast-until-rise-working-man-180954041/
- https://www.worldhistory.org/article/684/food-in-the-roman-world/
- https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Roman_cuisine

**Confidence: medium-high.** Yeldham's quote is widely cited and appears on BBC; the distinction between philosophical ideal and actual practice is well-attested. The claim "one meal a day" as an aspirational norm is defensible; calling it a behavioral fact is an overclaim.

**Caption-safe: yes** — e.g., "ROMANS: ONE MEAL / A DAY" or label "CENA — THE ONLY REAL MEAL" is safe if contextualized as a belief, not a law.

## 2. Medieval European Two-Meal Pattern

**Claim:** Medieval Europeans typically ate two meals a day — a main dinner around 10:30–11 am and a lighter supper about five hours later — and breakfast was considered morally suspect.

**Detail:** Historians Ian Mortimer and Caroline Yeldham confirm this two-meal schedule. The Smithsonian's coverage of their work specifies "a main meal around 10:30 or 11 in the morning and a second meal about five hours later." Breakfast was not practiced by most adults: "moralists frowned on breaking the overnight fast too early." Exceptions were working men (manual laborers, harvest workers who were owed breakfast by manor lords), young children, the elderly, and the sick. Even monarchs, whose daily activities were extensively documented, did not regularly eat breakfast before the 16th century.

**Source URLs:**
- https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/mostly-old-and-ill-ate-breakfast-until-rise-working-man-180954041/
- https://timothyrjeveland.com/medieval-breakfast/

**Confidence: high.** Supported by multiple food historians citing primary medieval sources. The ~10:30–11 am timing for "dinner" is specific and consistent across sources.

**Caption-safe: yes** — e.g., "DINNER: 11AM" or "TWO MEALS / PER DAY" works cleanly.

## 3. Aquinas and Breakfast as Gluttony

**Claim:** In his *Summa Theologica* (c. 1265–1274), Thomas Aquinas identified *praepropere* — eating too soon after waking — as one of five specific forms of gluttony, making breakfast a potential mortal sin under medieval Catholic theology.

**Detail:** Aquinas listed five sub-species of gluttony in *Summa Theologica* (II-II, Q.148): *laute* (eating too expensively), *studiose* (eating too daintily), *nimis* (eating too much), *praepropere* (eating too soon/at the wrong time), and *ardenter* (eating too greedily). *Praepropere* specifically condemned eating before the appropriate hour — which medieval custom placed at around midday for the main meal. The 1513 *famousformydinnerparties.com* gloss and Wikipedia's gluttony article both confirm this taxonomy. This is genuinely from Aquinas; the claim is well-sourced.

**Source URLs:**
- https://www.famousformydinnerparties.com/all/2021/05/10/five-kinds-of-gluttony
- https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gluttony
- https://www.historyhit.com/what-did-we-eat-for-breakfast-before-cereal/

**Confidence: high.** The Aquinas taxonomy is canonical and widely confirmed. The application to breakfast specifically follows directly from the text.

**Caption-safe: yes** — "PRAEPROPERE — SIN OF EATING TOO SOON" or "AQUINAS, 1265" works as a dated label.

## 4. Industrial Revolution and Fixed Mealtimes

**Claim:** The Industrial Revolution — by removing workers from home kitchens and imposing clock-driven factory shifts — created the modern three-meal schedule: a quick early breakfast before the shift, a constrained midday break (inventing "lunch"), and a larger family dinner in the evening.

**Detail:** Food historian Abigail Carroll documents this in *Three Squares: The Invention of the American Meal* (Basic Books, 2013). Before industrialization, American colonists ate "according to the agrarian organization of a day" — a flexible schedule tied to agricultural rhythms, with dinner as the main midday meal and supper in the evening. Factory work meant workers could no longer return home for a midday meal; this invented the portable, bought, or packed "lunch." Dinner shifted to the evening and became "an important site for family gathering" in the industrial city. A 1515 English statute requiring workers to start at 5 am already anchored breakfast for laborers.

**Source URLs:**
- https://www.hachettebookgroup.com/titles/abigail-carroll/three-squares/9780465025527/
- https://lit.newcity.com/2013/10/30/nonfiction-review-three-squares-the-invention-of-the-american-meal-by-abigail-carroll/
- https://www.wpr.org/shows/larry-meiller-show/three-squares-invention-american-meal

**Confidence: high.** Carroll's book is peer-reviewed food history from Basic Books; this is the authoritative source on American meal structure. The specific mechanisms (distance from home, factory clock) are consistently reported.

**Caption-safe: yes** — "1800s: FACTORY SHIFTS / INVENT LUNCH" or "DINNER MOVES TO EVENING" both work.

## 5. Kellogg Corn Flakes — Invention vs. Commercialization

**Claim:** John Harvey Kellogg accidentally invented wheat flakes at the Battle Creek Sanitarium in 1894; corn flakes specifically appeared by 1898; his brother Will Kellogg founded the commercial cereal company (Battle Creek Toasted Corn Flake Company) on February 19, 1906.

**Detail:** The accidental origin story: a batch of boiled wheat was left out overnight, then rolled and toasted, producing flakes. The 1894 date appears in most secondary sources for the original wheat-flake Granose. EBSCO Research Starters and Wikipedia's corn flakes article confirm the corn version appeared by 1898; a longer-shelf-life version by 1902. Will Kellogg added sugar and malt for commercial appeal and founded the company February 19, 1906. John Harvey Kellogg's primary motivations were Seventh-day Adventist health principles (vegetarianism, digestive health, anti-stimulant diet) — the popular "anti-masturbation" framing is a reductive but partially true gloss: his broader goal was suppressing all "carnal" appetite through bland food. Snopes rates the masturbation-specific claim as "mixed" (real philosophy, likely not the *explicit* purpose of corn flakes).

**Source URLs:**
- https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/kelloggs-corn-flakes-launch-dry-cereal-industry
- https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corn_flakes
- https://www.history.com/articles/cereal-breakfast-origins-kellogg

**Confidence: high** for 1906 commercial founding. **Medium** for 1894 as the wheat-flake invention date (some sources say "late 1890s" for corn flakes specifically; the 1894 date is wheat-based Granose, not corn flakes per se). Use "1890s" in script to cover both without overclaiming.

**Caption-safe: yes** — "CORN FLAKES, 1890s" or "KELLOGG'S COMPANY, 1906" are both defensible.

## 6. Beech-Nut Bacon-and-Eggs Campaign (Bernays)

**Claim:** In the early 1920s, PR pioneer Edward Bernays was hired by the Beech-Nut Packing Company to boost bacon sales. He arranged for a doctor to survey ~5,000 physicians on whether a heavier breakfast was beneficial; ~4,500 responded positively. Bernays distributed the result to newspapers as "4,500 physicians urge Americans to eat heavy breakfasts," and bacon-and-eggs became the canonical American breakfast.

**Detail:** The 5,000/4,500 discrepancy is explained by the campaign itself: Bernays asked a doctor to write to 5,000 physician contacts; approximately 4,500 responded affirmatively. The headlines reported the 4,500 respondents, not the 5,000 solicited. The year is reported as 1922 in at least one source (TodayIFoundOut, citing campaign start date). Bernays describes the general strategy in his 1928 book *Propaganda* (the doctors-as-trusted-advisors mechanism) and is believed to have described the Beech-Nut campaign specifically in his 1965 memoir *Biography of an Idea*. However: the campaign year is inconsistently reported (1920s generally; 1922 in one source) and NO currently accessible primary source (newspaper archive, Bernays papers) has been independently verified in this research session. Secondary sources all trace back to Bernays' own accounts, making this a self-reported campaign with high plausibility but no external archival confirmation found in this research.

**Source URLs:**
- https://www.todayifoundout.com/index.php/2022/06/how-two-guys-are-the-reason-we-eat-what-we-eat-for-breakfast/
- https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_relations_campaigns_of_Edward_Bernays
- https://www.thisiscapitalism.org/blog/bacon-eggs-and-public-relations

**Confidence: medium.** The campaign is widely reported and Bernays himself described similar mechanics in *Propaganda* (1928). The specific number 4,500 and year 1922 appear in secondary literature citing Bernays' memoir. No newspaper archive or independent verification found in this session. The *mechanism* is highly credible (physician endorsement PR); the *specific detail* (exactly 4,500) should be softened.

**Caption-safe: conditional** — "BEECH-NUT, 1920s" and "4,500 DOCTORS AGREE" work as narrative devices but should be flagged as Bernays' self-reported account. Do NOT state the year as a hard fact.

## 7. "Breakfast Is the Most Important Meal of the Day" as Marketing

**Claim:** The phrase "breakfast is the most important meal of the day" was coined in 1917 by Lenna F. Cooper in an article for *Good Health* magazine — a publication edited by John Harvey Kellogg — not by a nutritional authority operating independently of commercial interest.

**Detail:** Lenna F. Cooper wrote in the August 1917 issue of *Good Health*: "in many ways, the breakfast is the most important meal of the day, because it is the meal that gets the day started." Cooper was the first vice president of the American Dietetic Association (co-founded 1917) and had been mentored by Kellogg at the Battle Creek Sanitarium. *Good Health* was published by the Sanitarium, which had a commercial interest in cereal. The phrase spread further in the 1940s through a General Foods (Grape-Nuts) nationwide ad campaign — "Eat a Good Breakfast—Do a Better Job" — which deployed radio ads explicitly using "nutrition experts say breakfast is the most important meal of the day." Good Housekeeping ran a similar sentiment as early as 1889, so the *idea* predates Cooper, but the exact phrase is credibly traced to her 1917 piece.

**Source URLs:**
- https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lenna_F._Cooper
- https://www.meatrition.com/history/breakfast-lenna-cooper-myth
- https://www.huffpost.com/entry/breakfast-most-important-history_n_5910054

**Confidence: high** that Cooper's 1917 article is the documented first use of the phrase. **High** that it appeared in a Kellogg-affiliated publication. The Grape-Nuts 1940s expansion is also well-attested.

**Caption-safe: yes** — "GOOD HEALTH MAGAZINE, 1917" or "COINED IN A CEREAL COMPANY'S MAGAZINE" are accurate and punchy.

## 8. Circadian Metabolism — Morning vs. Evening Insulin Sensitivity

**Claim (a):** The human body processes glucose significantly better in the morning than in the evening — a peer-reviewed 2015 PNAS study found postprandial (after-meal) glucose was 17% higher at 8 pm than at 8 am, driven by lower β-cell insulin response (27% lower early-phase insulin) in the biological evening.

**Detail:** Morris et al. (2015) in *PNAS* conducted an 8-day controlled inpatient study with 14 healthy adults (mean age 28, BMI 25.4). Key quantitative findings: postprandial glucose 17% higher at biological evening (8 pm) vs morning (8 am); early-phase insulin 27% lower in the biological evening; circadian misalignment itself (12-hour behavioral inversion) added an additional 6% increase in postprandial glucose, via decreased insulin sensitivity. Important caveats: n=14 healthy young adults; only two time points measured; short misalignment exposure; results may not generalize to diabetics or shift workers.

**Claim (b):** Late eating that overlaps with peak melatonin secretion impairs glucose tolerance, with the effect amplified in carriers of the MTNR1B risk gene variant.

**Detail:** Garaulet and Scheer's ONTIME-MT randomized crossover trial (published in *Diabetes Care*) found late-evening high melatonin levels concurrent with eating impaired glucose tolerance; this was amplified in homozygous carriers of MTNR1B rs10830963. Earlier work (Rubio-Sastre et al., *Sleep* 2014) showed acute exogenous melatonin impairs glucose tolerance in both morning and evening. The mechanism: melatonin inhibits insulin secretion from pancreatic beta cells.

**Claim (c):** Early time-restricted eating (eTRE) — consuming all calories within an 8-10 hour daytime window — improves metabolic markers in human trials.

**Detail:** Sutton et al. (2018, *Cell Metabolism*) found that 5-week eTRE (6 am–3 pm eating window) in men with prediabetes improved insulin sensitivity, blood pressure, and oxidative stress *without weight loss*. Multiple subsequent RCTs and meta-analyses confirm ~20% spontaneous calorie reduction and ~3% body weight decrease from TRE. Most RCTs are short-duration (4–12 weeks) and involve overweight adults; long-term RCT evidence in healthy populations remains limited.

**Source URLs:**
- https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4418873/ (Morris et al. 2015 PNAS full text)
- https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0261561417301267 (Garaulet/Scheer late dinner study)
- https://www.cell.com/cell-metabolism/fulltext/S1550-4131(23)00454-0 (TRE review)

**Confidence: high** for the directional claim (morning > evening for glucose metabolism). **High** for the Sutton 2018 eTRE finding (replicated). The specific "17%" and "27%" figures should be attributed to "a 2015 PNAS study" rather than stated as universal facts, given the small n=14 sample.

**Caption-safe: yes for direction** — "YOUR BODY HANDLES FOOD BETTER IN THE MORNING" is safe. "17% WORSE GLUCOSE AT NIGHT" is true but should note it's from one study; use "STUDIES SHOW" prefix.

## 9. Homo sapiens Age (~300,000 Years)

**Claim:** Homo sapiens is approximately 300,000 years old, based on fossils discovered at Jebel Irhoud, Morocco, published in *Nature* in 2017.

**Detail:** Jean-Jacques Hublin and colleagues published findings in *Nature* (June 8, 2017) dating Jebel Irhoud fossils to ~315,000 ± 34,000 years ago using thermoluminescence dating. The Smithsonian Institution's Human Origins program states these are "the oldest members of our species yet found." The Smithsonian rounds to "at least 300,000 years." The NPR headline used "315,000-year-old." Importantly: not all paleoanthropologists unambiguously classify the Jebel Irhoud specimens as fully modern *Homo sapiens* — they show archaic features like a long, low braincase. John Hawks (paleoanthropologist, University of Wisconsin) notes the classification is debated. The Smithsonian's framing is the most authoritative institutional consensus: "at least 300,000 years."

**Source URLs:**
- https://humanorigins.si.edu/research/whats-hot-human-origins/our-species-arose-least-300000-years-ago
- https://www.nhm.ac.uk/discover/news/2017/june/oldest-known-homo-sapiens-fossils-discovered-in-morocco.html
- https://www.science.org/content/article/world-s-oldest-homo-sapiens-fossils-found-morocco

**Confidence: high** for "at least 300,000 years" as the institutional consensus figure. Saying "about 300,000 years" in a video is safe and accurate. Saying "exactly 300,000" is overclaiming.

**Caption-safe: yes** — "300,000 YEARS AGO" is accurate. "300,000 YEARS / NO BREAKFAST TIME" is fine as rhetorical framing.

## 10. Bonus Well-Sourced Facts

### 10a. "Dinner" etymologically means "break the fast" — just like breakfast

**Claim:** The word "dinner" comes from Old French *disner*, from Vulgar Latin *disiūnāre*, literally meaning "to break one's fast" — so "dinner" and "breakfast" are etymological synonyms, both meaning the same act at different times of day.

**Detail:** Etymonline and Wikipedia's Dinner article confirm: *dinner* enters English c. 1300 from Anglo-French *disner*, from Gallo-Roman *\*desiunare* ("to break one's fast"). The word originally named the *first substantial meal* of the day (eaten between 9 am and noon in medieval times) and only shifted to mean an evening meal as fashionable classes pushed mealtimes later through the 17th–19th centuries.

**Source URLs:**
- https://www.etymonline.com/word/dinner
- https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dinner

**Confidence: high** (philological fact, OED/Etymonline level).
**Caption-safe: yes** — "DINNER = BREAK THE FAST" is a genuine hook.

### 10b. "Breakfast" first appears in written English in 1463

**Claim:** The word "breakfast" appears in written English from 1463 (mid-15th century), combining Old English "break" + "fast."

**Detail:** Etymonline dates the noun "breakfast" to "mid-15c." with the OED's earliest evidence from 1463. Before this, Old English used *morgenmete* ("morning meal") and *undernmete*. The word "breakfast" itself encodes the moral/religious framing: the overnight fast was a virtue, and the morning meal was defined by its relationship to breaking that fast.

**Source URL:**
- https://www.etymonline.com/word/breakfast

**Confidence: high.**
**Caption-safe: yes** — "FIRST WRITTEN: 1463" works as a dated label.

### 10c. "Lunch" is the newest named meal — a product of industrialization

**Claim:** "Lunch" as a standalone mid-day meal is the newest of the three meals, emerging in the 19th century as industrial workers could no longer go home for the traditional midday "dinner."

**Detail:** The word "lunch" shortened from "luncheon" (first attested 1610s as "a light repast between mealtimes"). Before industrialization, the main midday meal was called "dinner." Carroll (*Three Squares*) specifically identifies the industrialization of American life as the cause of "dinner" moving to the evening and a new, lighter, portable midday "lunch" being invented. Jane Austen (early 19th century) still used "nuncheon" (from Middle English "noon drink") for a midday snack.

**Source URLs:**
- https://www.etymonline.com/word/lunch
- https://lit.newcity.com/2013/10/30/nonfiction-review-three-squares-the-invention-of-the-american-meal-by-abigail-carroll/

**Confidence: high** (Carroll is the authoritative source; etymology confirmed by Etymonline).
**Caption-safe: yes** — "LUNCH: THE NEWEST MEAL" is accurate.

### 10d. Native Americans ate with no fixed mealtimes; colonists judged this as uncivilized

**Claim:** Explorer John Smith recorded that Native Americans "have no such thing as set meals breakfast, dinner or supper" — they ate when hungry, varied by season, and sometimes fasted. English colonists viewed this as evidence of savagery.

**Detail:** Carroll (*Three Squares*) cites John Smith's observation. Colonists believed structured, clock-driven meals were a marker of civilization separating humans from animals. This is the colonial frame that ultimately imposed the three-meal structure on American culture.

**Source URLs:**
- https://www.goodreads.com/en/book/show/16193688-three-squares (Carroll summary)
- https://www.kirkusreviews.com/book-reviews/abigail-carroll/three-squares/

**Confidence: medium-high** (Carroll is citable; John Smith's exact quote is reported by Carroll but not independently verified in this session from Smith's primary text).
**Caption-safe: conditional** — "JOHN SMITH, 1600s: NO SET MEALS" requires attribution to Carroll citing Smith; don't present as a raw primary source.

### 10e. A 1515 English statute anchored laborers' breakfast by law

**Claim:** A 1515 English statute required laborers to begin work at 5 am, cementing breakfast as a necessary meal for the working class before the shift.

**Detail:** This is cited in the Smithsonian article on the history of breakfast, attributed to Ian Mortimer (historian, author of *The Time Traveler's Guide to Medieval England*).

**Source URL:**
- https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/mostly-old-and-ill-ate-breakfast-until-rise-working-man-180954041/

**Confidence: medium** (cited by reputable source attributing to Mortimer; primary statute text not verified in this session).
**Caption-safe: yes** — "LABOR LAW, 1515" works as a dated label if attributed to this historical claim.

## Discarded / Softened

### Bernays campaign year
No verified newspaper archive or independent archival source pins the Beech-Nut campaign to a specific year. "1922" appears in one secondary source (TodayIFoundOut); other sources say "1920s." **Script must say "in the 1920s"** not "in 1925" or any specific year. The 4,500/5,000 physician discrepancy should be resolved by noting: Bernays asked a doctor to contact 5,000 colleagues; ~4,500 responded positively; headlines reported the 4,500 respondents. The "4,500 physicians" framing is what ran in newspapers and is the more famous number.

### Kellogg "anti-masturbation" claim
The popular internet claim that corn flakes were "invented to prevent masturbation" is a reductive myth. John Harvey Kellogg did believe in suppressing carnal appetite through bland food, but Snopes rates the direct anti-masturbation-purpose claim as "mixed." **Script must frame it as: Kellogg believed bland, grain-based food would suppress all physical passions — sexual appetite included — as part of his broader Adventist health philosophy.** Do not say "invented to stop masturbation" as a clean fact.

### "Breakfast is the most important meal" as a Kellogg slogan
The phrase is often attributed directly to "Kellogg's" the company as a marketing line. More accurate: it was written by Lenna Cooper in a Kellogg-affiliated magazine in 1917 — Cooper was a nutritionist, not a copywriter, and sincerely believed it. The *marketing* amplification came in the 1940s via General Foods' Grape-Nuts campaign, not Kellogg's directly. **Script should say "coined in a cereal company's magazine" rather than "invented by Kellogg's marketing department."**

### John Harvey Kellogg wheat flakes vs corn flakes date
The 1894 date applies to wheat-based Granose; corn flakes specifically appeared by 1898. Saying "Kellogg invented corn flakes in 1894" is an error. **Script should say "in the 1890s" or "by 1898 for corn flakes specifically."**

### 300,000-year Homo sapiens claim
The Jebel Irhoud specimens are classified as early Homo sapiens by Hublin et al. and by the Smithsonian, but some paleoanthropologists dispute this classification given archaic cranial features. **Script should say "our species is at least 300,000 years old" — not "300,000 years ago humans evolved" — to match the institutional consensus without overstating certainty.**

### Circadian metabolism — specific percentages
The 17% higher glucose figure comes from a study of n=14 healthy young adults. This is robust enough to cite directionally but too small to present as a universal rule. **Script should say "studies show your body is significantly more efficient at handling food in the morning" and reserve the 17% figure for the on-screen text with "one study found" framing.**

### Native American eating / John Smith quote
Carroll cites Smith, but the exact Smith quote has not been verified from Smith's primary text in this session. **Script should attribute this to food historian Abigail Carroll citing colonial-era accounts, not directly to John Smith.**
